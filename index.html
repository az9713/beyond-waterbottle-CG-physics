What do the two ratios mean—and why does the surface stay fixed?

The water surface is the boundary between water and air. The combined CG is the balance point of all remaining mass. These are different locations. With the bottle interior held fixed, changing the bottle’s mass or its balance point does not add or remove water, so it does not move that boundary.

1. Specify the system before calculating

The system is the bottle plus water remaining inside it. Swallowed water is excluded. Compare settled upright states between sips, with uniform gravity, incompressible water, a rigid cylindrical interior, negligible air mass, and no retained films. A cap is part of the empty-bottle mass only while attached. Center of mass and center of gravity coincide in a uniform gravitational field. [1]

2. From a mass integral to a weighted average

A slice at height s has mass ρA ds. Integrating mass and its first moment gives: [2]

\[m(h)=\int_0^h\rho A\,ds=kh,\qquad J(h)=\int_0^h s\rho A\,ds=\frac{kh^2}{2}.\]

Here J is the water’s first moment of mass about the bottom. Its centroid is J/m=h/2. Therefore

\[z(h)=\frac{Mb+J(h)}{M+m(h)}=\boxed{\frac{Mb+kh^2/2}{M+kh}}.\]

This is a state calculation, not an energy-minimization principle. The normalization changes as water is removed.

3. Endpoints and the dip

\[z(0)=b,\qquad z(H)-b=\frac{kH}{M+kH}\left(\frac H2-b\right).\]

The full and empty CGs agree only when b=H/2. Under that assumption:

\[z(H)=z(0)=H/2,\qquad z(h)-\frac H2=-\frac{k h(H-h)}{2(M+kh)}<0\quad(0<h<H).\]

Every partially filled state is below the common endpoint. A heavy base or heavy neck changes the endpoints, not the weighted-average principle.

4. A differential identity with a physical meaning

\[\frac{dz}{dh}=\frac{k\left(Mh+kh^2/2-Mb\right)}{(M+kh)^2}=\boxed{\frac{k}{M+kh}[h-z(h)]}.\]

During drinking, dh/dt is negative. Hence a water surface above the combined CG implies a falling CG; a surface below it implies a rising CG. The criterion uses the location of the layer missing between settled states, even when a straw withdraws water from the bottom.

\[\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{k[h-z(h)]}{M+kh}\frac{dh}{dt}.\]

5. Solve the minimum and prove it is unique

Let h* denote the critical water depth and z_min the minimum CG height. Set the derivative to zero:

\[\frac{k}{2}h_*^2+Mh_*-Mb=0,\qquad h_*=\frac{\sqrt{M^2+2kMb}-M}{k}=\frac{2Mb}{\sqrt{M^2+2kMb}+M}.\]

The second expression avoids subtracting nearly equal numbers when the bottle is very heavy. At the minimum:

\[\boxed{z_{\min}=h_*}.\]

For 0<b<H, the derivative’s numerator increases strictly from a negative value at the bottom to a positive value at the top. A stronger check is global convexity:

\[\frac{d^2z}{dh^2}=\frac{kM(M+2kb)}{(M+kh)^3}>0.\]

Thus there is exactly one interior minimum. At b=0, the minimum is the empty endpoint; at M=0, the empty system has no defined CG and the usual return disappears.

6. Dimensionless form and asymptotics

\[y(f)=\frac{r\beta+f^2/2}{r+f},\qquad f_* = \sqrt{r^2+2r\beta}-r=\frac{2r\beta}{\sqrt{r^2+2r\beta}+r},\qquad y_{\min}=f_*.\]

For a midpoint-balanced shell, β=1/2:

\[y(f)=\frac{r+f^2}{2(r+f)},\qquad f_*=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/r}+1}.\]
\[r\to0^+:\ f_*\sim\sqrt r;\qquad r\to\infty:\ f_*=\frac12-\frac{1}{8r}+O(r^{-2}).\]

A light bottle descends far before its shell regains control. A heavy bottle scarcely changes CG. The minimum is strictly below half-full for any finite positive r when β=1/2.

7. Time is a separate model

Let q(t)≥0 be the water mass removed per unit time, and m_full the initial water mass. Then

\[m(t)=m_{\rm full}-\int_0^t q(s)\,ds,\qquad f(t)=\frac{m(t)}{m_{\rm full}},\qquad z(t)=H y(f(t)).\]

For constant q and total duration T=m_full/q, f=1−τ. Pauses give plateaus, and changes in rate stretch the same state curve horizontally. They cannot create a new settled turning point while fill decreases monotonically.

\[\frac{z(t)}H=\frac{r\beta+(1-\tau)^2/2}{r+1-\tau},\qquad t_*/T=1-f_*.\]

Discover the result without algebra

Three arguments answer three different questions: must a dip exist, why can the CG rise, and where can the minimum occur? None requires tracking fluid particles.

1. The endpoints force a dip

A full symmetric cylinder balances at mid-height. An empty midpoint-balanced shell also balances there. With any partial fill, the water balances below mid-height. Their combined balance point lies between the water’s and the shell’s, so it is below mid-height.

Every intermediate state is lower than both endpoints. The journey must descend and return.

This proves that a minimum exists. It does not, by itself, prove there is only one. The uniqueness proof needs the ordered-layer argument in the mathematics tab.

2. Vanishing ballast resolves the paradox

Replace the water in your mind with a single equivalent weight at its own balance point. As you drink, that weight moves downward and becomes lighter. Initially its downward motion matters most; near empty, its disappearance matters most.

Remove low ballast from a bottle and its balance point rises—even if no material moves upward.

A center of gravity is a location computed from the mass distribution. It is not a marked particle that must travel with some piece of matter.

3. Half-full cannot be the minimum

At half-full, the water surface is at the bottle’s midpoint. The water’s balance point is below it, so the combined CG is below it too. The next sip removes an effective top layer above the combined CG and therefore lowers it further.

For a uniform cylinder whose empty shell balances at mid-height, the minimum must occur with less than half the water remaining. The mass ratio determines how much less.

4. The marginal-layer test

Imagine removing a thin wafer from the settled water distribution. Removing a wafer above the balance point lowers the balance point. Removing one below raises it. The wafer has no first-order effect exactly when its height equals the current balance point.

The wafer describes the difference between two settled states. The actual water molecules may follow complicated paths through a straw; that history does not affect the final mass distribution under these assumptions.

5. A geometric lever picture

The empty shell pulls the combined CG toward its fixed balance point. The water pulls it toward a lower balance point. The displacement below the empty-shell CG is the water’s share of total mass multiplied by the separation between those two balance points. One factor shrinks during drinking, while the other grows. Their competition creates the dip.

7. Can symmetry give the answer? Yes—but symmetry of what?

Symmetry can locate the endpoints and, together with positivity of mass and continuity, establish a dip. It cannot by itself locate the minimum, prove uniqueness, or make the drinking trajectory symmetric in time. The key distinction is between symmetry of a mass distribution, symmetry of a first moment, and symmetry of a normalized center of gravity.

The short argument without mathematics

Pair every mass element in the upper half of a full uniform water column with an equal element equally far below the midpoint. Their moments cancel. Do the same for a reflection-symmetric shell. The full bottle therefore balances at the midpoint; the empty shell does too.

With a partial fill, the water column has its own reflection plane halfway up that shorter column, below the bottle midpoint. Its CG is therefore below the shell’s. The combined CG lies between them, so it is below the midpoint. As the fill changes continuously from full to empty, the CG must dip below the common endpoints and return.

That is a symmetry-assisted proof of the existence of a minimum. The ingredients beyond symmetry are positive masses, the weighted-average property, and continuity. A midpoint CG of the shell is enough for the result; literal top–bottom shell symmetry is a sufficient condition, not a necessary one.

Why reflecting a half-empty bottle does not solve the problem

Reflect a partially filled upright bottle across its midpoint plane. Water originally at the bottom now occupies the top, leaving air underneath. That is not another settled upright state under the same downward gravity. The allowed settled configurations are not closed under this reflection. Rotating the bottle upside down is also a different operation: the liquid redistributes, and the shell’s mass distribution need not be top–bottom symmetric.

Even in the full and empty cases, the argument uses symmetry of the mass distribution. The complete gravitational experiment is not invariant under a horizontal reflection while the downward gravitational field is held fixed. Distribution symmetry is enough to locate the mass centroid in uniform gravity; a symmetry of the full dynamics is not required.

The subtler symmetry: equal moment, unequal CG

For two complementary settled fills—for example one-quarter full and three-quarters full—the total mass moment about the bottle’s midpoint is the same. One way to see this: the bottom quarter has the opposite moment to its reflected top quarter. Since the complete water column has zero midpoint moment, the bottom three-quarters must have the same moment as the bottom quarter.

But the quarter-full bottle has less total mass. The same negative moment divided by a smaller mass places its CG farther below the midpoint. Thus a quarter-full bottle balances lower than its three-quarter-full counterpart. The symmetric quantity is the unnormalized moment, not the CG.

The numerator has a complementary-fill symmetry. Dividing by a fill-dependent total mass breaks that symmetry in the CG.
Optional mathematical proof: reflection, complementary fills, and broken symmetry

Measure vertical position from the bottle midpoint: let ξ=s−H/2, where s is height above the bottom and H is bottle height. Let dm denote an element of mass. A distribution invariant under ξ→−ξ has an odd first-moment integrand, so

\[\int \xi\,dm=0,\qquad z_{CG}=H/2.\]

For the uniform cylindrical water column, retain k=ρA as water mass per unit height, h as current depth, M as shell mass, and b=H/2 as shell CG height. Define Q(h) as the combined first moment about the midpoint plane. The shell contributes zero; the water gives

\[Q(h)=\int_0^h(s-H/2)k\,ds=-\frac{k}{2}h(H-h),\qquad \boxed{Q(H-h)=Q(h)}.\]

Let f=h/H be the fill fraction, r=M/(kH)>0 the shell/full-water mass ratio, and y=z/H the normalized CG height. Dividing Q by the total mass gives

\[y(f)-\frac12=\frac{Q(fH)}{H(M+kHf)}=-\frac{f(1-f)}{2(r+f)}.\]

The product f(1−f) is unchanged under f→1−f. The denominator r+f is not. In fact,

\[\boxed{y(f)-y(1-f)=-\frac{f(1-f)(1-2f)}{2(r+f)(r+1-f)}}.\]

For 0<f<1/2, this is strictly negative. Thus a global minimum cannot lie above half-full: its complementary state below half-full would have a lower CG. To exclude half-full itself without calculus, make a small downward change in f. The moment magnitude changes only quadratically, while total mass decreases linearly. The dip therefore initially deepens.

Precisely, let ε be a positive dimensionless amount smaller than 1/2, and define the normalized dip D(f)=1/2−y(f). Then

\[\frac{D(1/2-\varepsilon)}{D(1/2)}=\frac{(1-4\varepsilon^2)(r+1/2)}{r+1/2-\varepsilon}>1\quad\text{if}\quad0<\varepsilon<\min\!\left\{\frac12,\frac{1}{4(r+1/2)}\right\}.\]

Consequently the minimum is strictly below half-full. This uses complementary-moment symmetry plus the normalization by mass; symmetry alone is insufficient. The unique location still follows from the turning-point or convexity argument in the full mathematics chapter:

\[f_*=\sqrt{r^2+r}-r,\qquad 0<f_*<1/2.\]

Nor is there a hidden exact reflection symmetry about f*. Such a symmetry would make every odd derivative vanish there. For this midpoint-shell cylinder, the third derivative with respect to f is

\[\frac{d^3y}{df^3}=-\frac{3r(r+1)}{(r+f)^4}\ne0\qquad(r>0).\]

There is an approximate quadratic symmetry sufficiently close to the minimum, as for any smooth nondegenerate minimum, but no exact mirror symmetry of the full curve.

What survives if the shell is not midpoint-balanced?

Let β=b/H denote the shell’s normalized CG height. Its midpoint moment is M H(β−1/2), which is constant as water is removed. Therefore the complementary-moment identity Q(H−h)=Q(h) still holds for a cylindrical interior, even though the full and empty CGs no longer coincide:

\[\frac{Q(fH)}{kH^2}=r(\beta-1/2)-\frac12 f(1-f).\]

The sign of this common moment can now vary with fill and shell balance, so the lower-fill-versus-higher-fill ordering and the less-than-half-full minimum are no longer universal. A heavy-top shell can place the minimum above half-full. Shell reflection symmetry, midpoint balance, and cylindrical geometry must not be silently interchanged.

Answer to “symmetry about what?”: rotations about the vertical axis fix the lateral position; reflection about a horizontal midplane fixes the full/empty vertical centroids when the mass distributions permit it; reflection of the fill parameter f about 1/2 preserves the centered first moment. None is a symmetry of the complete normalized CG trajectory.

What these arguments do not justify

They do not imply that gravity chooses a low-CG drinking trajectory, that ordinary drinking is upright, or that CG height alone predicts resistance to every disturbance. Those are separate questions.

Start with measurable geometry and shell balance, then add fluid dynamics only when the measurement demands it. A more elaborate model is useful when it changes a prediction you can test.

1. General cross-section: the exact rigid-container theorem

Let A(s)>0 be horizontal interior area at height s, and retain uniform water density ρ, shell mass M, and fixed shell CG b. Define m(h) as water mass and J(h) as its first moment:

\[m(h)=\rho\int_0^h A(s)\,ds,\qquad J(h)=\rho\int_0^h sA(s)\,ds,\qquad z(h)=\frac{Mb+J(h)}{M+m(h)}.\]
\[\frac{dz}{dh}=\frac{\rho A(h)}{M+m(h)}[h-z(h)].\]

To prove uniqueness, define N(h)=(M+m(h))(h−z(h)). Then

\[N(h)=M(h-b)+\rho\int_0^h(h-s)A(s)\,ds,\qquad N^{\prime}(h)=M+m(h)>0.\]

For b strictly between the lowest and highest fluid-accessible elevations, N starts negative and ends positive. Hence the settled vertical CG has one interior minimum. The critical depth satisfies h*=z(h*). Geometry changes where it occurs, including the fraction of water remaining.

Refinement of the earlier discussion: fixed tilt alone does not destroy this vertical-CG theorem. At a fixed orientation, describe the container by horizontal slices in the laboratory frame. Changing orientation during drinking, trapped compartments, capillarity, or motion can break the required one-parameter description.

2. An analytic shape family you can explore

Let u=h/H be dimensionless depth, a be an area-slope parameter with −1<a<1, and A₀ the mean cross-sectional area. Set A(Hu)=A₀[1+a(2u−1)]. Let V(u) be volume divided by full volume and Ĵ(u) the water moment divided by full-water mass times H.

\[V(u)=(1-a)u+a u^2,\qquad \widehat J(u)=\frac{1-a}{2}u^2+\frac{2a}{3}u^3.\]
\[\frac zH=\frac{r\beta+\widehat J(u)}{r+V(u)},\qquad f=V(u),\qquad u_* = \frac{r\beta+\widehat J(u_*)}{r+V(u_*)}.\]

Positive a means a broader top; negative a means a broader base. The interface solves the last equation by bisection, using its unique root. The schematic is an axisymmetric container cross-section with width proportional to the square root of area.

3. Air: an exact small correction

Let ρw and ρa be constant water and air densities. Let V₀ be full internal volume, z_v its geometric centroid, and V(h), J_v(h) the occupied-water volume and its volume first moment. Replace an all-air interior with water in the occupied region:

\[M_e=M+\rho_a V_0,\qquad M_e b_e=Mb+\rho_a V_0z_v,\qquad \Delta\rho=\rho_w-\rho_a.\]
\[z(h)=\frac{M_e b_e+\Delta\rho\,J_v(h)}{M_e+\Delta\rho\,V(h)}.\]

The same framework survives, with a modified baseline and density contrast. For room-condition estimates, ρa/ρw is of order 10⁻³. Actual values depend on temperature and pressure. This model assumes venting and approximately uniform air density; suction and a sealed flexible bottle need a different pressure–deformation model.

4. Retained films, droplets, and moving shell material

Let h be a state parameter, m_p(h) the pooled-water mass, J_p(h) its first moment, m_f(h) a retained-film mass, z_f(h) its centroid, and b(h) the shell CG for a shell of constant mass M. Primes below mean derivatives with respect to h. Define D and P as total mass and total first moment:

\[D=M+m_p+m_f,\quad P=Mb(h)+J_p+m_fz_f,\quad z=P/D.\]
\[z^{\prime}=\frac{Mb^{\prime}+m_p^{\prime}(h-z)+m_f^{\prime}(z_f-z)+m_fz_f^{\prime}}D,\qquad J_p^{\prime}=h m_p^{\prime}.\]

The extra terms show exactly how the simple threshold can fail. Shell motion, film detachment, and changing film position can add turning points. If a discrete droplet of mass δm at height z_d is removed, let D be the mass before removal and z_before its CG:

\[z_{\rm after}-z_{\rm before}=\frac{\delta m\,(z_{\rm before}-z_d)}{D-\delta m}.\]

Removing a neck droplet above the current CG produces a downward jump. A film may also make the state history-dependent: the same pooled depth can coexist with different retained-water distributions.

5. Tilt: solve an occupied three-dimensional domain

Let Ω be the interior in bottle-fixed coordinates r, Q a rotation into the laboratory frame, r₀ a translation, e_z the laboratory upward unit vector, and ℓ the laboratory water-surface elevation. The occupied region is Ωw(ℓ,Q).

\[\Omega_w(\ell,Q)=\{\mathbf r\in\Omega:\ \mathbf e_z\!\cdot(\mathbf r_0+Q\mathbf r)\leq\ell\}.\]

Choose ℓ so its volume equals the prescribed water volume V. Let r_b be the shell CG in bottle coordinates. The laboratory CG is

\[\mathbf r_{CG}=\mathbf r_0+Q\,\frac{M\mathbf r_b+\rho\int_{\Omega_w}\mathbf r\,dV}{M+\rho V}.\]

A changing Q adds orientation-dependent terms, so fill alone no longer determines the path. For a fixed Q, vertical slices recover the theorem above. A voxel integration or mesh-plane clipping algorithm can solve the static geometry; it is not a fluid-motion simulation.

6. Capillarity: when the horizontal-surface approximation fails

Define surface tension γ, gravitational acceleration g, representative liquid length L, and density contrast Δρ. The capillary length ℓ_c and Bond number Bo compare gravity with surface tension: [3]

\[\ell_c=\sqrt{\frac\gamma{\Delta\rho g}},\qquad Bo=\frac{\Delta\rho g L^2}{\gamma}=\left(\frac L{\ell_c}\right)^2.\]

For water near room temperature, γ≈0.072 N/m and Δρ≈1000 kg/m³ give ℓ_c≈2.7 mm. A centimeter-scale pool is gravity-dominated in its bulk, but thin films, small droplets, and corners can remain capillary-dominated. Use local liquid dimensions, not only the bottle radius.

For a static interface, define p_l and p_g as liquid and gas pressures, and signed curvature κ so that a spherical liquid droplet has κ=2/R_d, where R_d is droplet radius. Then

\[p_l-p_g=\gamma\kappa,\qquad \nabla p_l=\rho\mathbf g.\]

Together with contact-angle and contact-line conditions, these determine the interface. Advancing/receding contact-angle differences introduce hysteresis. Surface forces affect CG through the resulting mass distribution; they are not an extra mass in the CG formula.

7. Sloshing: a dynamical extension, not a corrected static curve

Let q be a displacement coordinate for a dominant slosh mode, ω its natural angular frequency, ζ its damping ratio, Γ a modal coupling factor, and a_x(t) the container’s horizontal acceleration. A reduced, small-amplitude model is: [4]

\[\ddot q+2\zeta\omega\dot q+\omega^2q=-\Gamma a_x(t).\]

Let r_w0 be the settled water centroid, e_x a horizontal unit vector, and c₂ a dimensionless geometry-dependent coefficient. For a symmetric tank and a dominant antisymmetric lateral mode, a schematic expansion is

\[\mathbf r_w=\mathbf r_{w0}+q\mathbf e_x+c_2\frac{q^2}{H}\mathbf e_z+\cdots,\qquad \mathbf r_{CG}=\frac{M\mathbf r_b+m\mathbf r_w}{M+m}.\]

The horizontal centroid can move at first order while its vertical shift begins at second order by symmetry. This expansion is illustrative: ω, ζ, Γ, and c₂ require geometry-specific analysis or measurement and vary with fill. It is not a calibrated slosh prediction.

8. The full fluid model and what closes it

For a Newtonian incompressible liquid, define velocity field v, pressure p, dynamic viscosity μ, and body acceleration g. In an inertial frame:

\[\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf v=0,\qquad \rho\left(\partial_t\mathbf v+\mathbf v\!\cdot\nabla\mathbf v\right)=-\nabla p+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf v+\rho\mathbf g.\]

A complete free-boundary problem also needs initial fluid shape and velocity, wall motion and no-slip conditions, free-surface kinematics, gas pressure and surface-stress conditions, outlet flux, and contact-line physics. The CG is then an integral over the evolving occupied liquid domain. For a deformable bottle, couple fluid traction to a structural constitutive law. These equations formulate the extension; this site does not claim to solve coupled CFD or fluid–structure interaction.

9. Density gradients and a systematic perturbation expansion

A fixed height-dependent density profile can be absorbed into a mass-per-height function λ(s)=ρ(s)A(s). Replace ρA by λ in the integrals and the same derivative and uniqueness theorem follow. If mixing or draining changes ρ throughout the occupied region, that fixed-profile assumption fails.

Let ε be a small dimensionless perturbation, D₀ and P₀ baseline total mass and first moment, and δD and δP their linear perturbation coefficients. Write D=D₀+εδD, P=P₀+εδP, and z₀=P₀/D₀. Then

\[z=z_0+\varepsilon\frac{\delta P-z_0\delta D}{D_0}+\varepsilon^2\frac{z_0(\delta D)^2-\delta P\delta D}{D_0^2}+O(\varepsilon^3).\]

This is a genuine first- and second-order expansion, rather than a list of loosely named second-order effects. A density perturbation δλ(s) at fixed depth contributes δD=∫δλ ds and δP=∫sδλ ds. Its first-order CG change is weighted by displacement from the original CG:

\[\delta z=\frac{1}{D_0}\int_0^h(s-z_0)\,\delta\lambda(s)\,ds.\]

Added density above the CG raises it; added density below lowers it. A shape or density perturbation can be assessed without first rerunning a complete dynamic model.

10. A gravity-gradient correction—and its limitation

In an ideal parallel field whose downward magnitude is g(s)=g₀(1−αs), let g₀ be the bottom gravity magnitude and α a small inverse-length gradient with αH≪1. Let angle brackets denote averages over the complete mass distribution, including the shell. The height of the gravity-force-weighted centroid is

\[z_g=\frac{\langle s(1-\alpha s)\rangle}{\langle1-\alpha s\rangle}=z_{CM}-\alpha\left(\langle s^2\rangle-\langle s\rangle^2\right)+O(\alpha^2H^3).\]

The bracketed quantity is vertical mass variance. A field weakening upward weights lower material more strongly. For a near-Earth estimate α≈2/R_E, where R_E≈6.37×10⁶ m is Earth’s radius. Since variance is at most H²/4 for a distribution confined to height H, a 30 cm bottle has a correction bounded at this order by about 7 nm. It is negligible compared with films or geometry errors. A fully nonparallel field generally cannot be represented by one universal CG point for all orientations; this formula is a parallel-field approximation, not a general definition.

1. Derive the universal balance law

Let W₀>0 be the initial weight, Y₀ the initial response, x added weight, and U(x) the response carried by its next increment. Let P(x) be the accumulated numerator. The integration variable s measures added weight.

\[P(x)=W_0Y_0+\int_0^xU(s)\,ds,\quad W(x)=W_0+x,\quad Y(x)=\frac{P(x)}{W(x)}.\]
\[\boxed{\frac{dY}{dx}=\frac{U(x)-Y(x)}{W_0+x}}.\]

Adding an increment pulls the average toward its value. The turning point is U=Y. Removing an identified increment reverses the sign. This is a balance identity, not necessarily a relaxation in time.

2. Exact bottle-shaped curves require a linear marginal input

Choose reference added weight X>0 and response scale Y_ref>0. Measure responses relative to a fixed baseline so U(0)=0. Define f=x/X, r=W₀/X, y=Y/Y_ref, and y₀=Y₀/Y_ref. Set U(x)=Y_ref x/X.

\[y(f)=\frac{r y_0+f^2/2}{r+f},\quad \frac{dy}{df}=\frac{f-y}{r+f},\quad f_*=\sqrt{r^2+2r y_0}-r.\]

Set y₀=1/2 and r=1/8 to recover the plotted example. The common minimum is f*=y_min=1/4. With a different marginal input the broad law survives, but the exact rational curve generally does not.

3. Thermal mixing

Let C₀ be initial heat capacity, T₀ initial equilibrium temperature relative to a reference, x added heat capacity, and T_in(x) inlet temperature relative to that same reference. Constant heat capacities and an insulated, nonreacting system with negligible work give: [5]

\[T(x)=\frac{C_0T_0+\int_0^xT_{in}(s)\,ds}{C_0+x}.\]

Progressively warmer feed can still cool the contents while it remains colder than the current mixture. The minimum occurs when inlet and mixture temperatures coincide. Example: C₀=100 J/K, X=800 J/K, reference temperature 20°C, response scale 40 K, initial temperature 40°C, and inlet temperature 20°C+40 K·f. The mixture reaches 30°C at f=1/4.

Correction to a loose earlier analogy: removing an aliquot from a well-mixed equilibrium liquid does not change its temperature. “Removing cold material warms the remainder” requires selectively removing a genuinely colder component before equilibration, not ordinary withdrawal from a uniform mixture.

4. Electrical charge sharing

Let C₀ and V₀ be initial capacitance and voltage, x added capacitance, and V_in(x) the initial voltage of each independently precharged incoming increment. Keep the assembly isolated from batteries and allow transients to settle. Charge conservation gives: [6]

\[V(x)=\frac{C_0V_0+\int_0^x V_{in}(s)\,ds}{C_0+x}.\]

Example: C₀=1 μF, X=8 μF, initial voltage 6 V, and incoming voltage 12 V·f. The common voltage reaches 3 V at f=1/4. These are limiting infinitesimal additions; finite capacitor steps give a discrete approximation. Charge is conserved, while stored electrical energy can dissipate in the interconnections.

5. Inelastic capture and momentum

Let M₀,v₀ denote initial cart mass and laboratory velocity; m accumulated captured mass; and U(m) incoming horizontal laboratory velocity. With negligible external horizontal impulse and complete capture: [7]

\[v(m)=\frac{M_0v_0+\int_0^m U(s)\,ds}{M_0+m},\qquad (M_0+m)\frac{dv}{dm}=U-v.\]

Example: M₀=2 kg, reference captured mass 16 kg, initial speed 3 m/s, and incoming speed 6 m/s·f. The minimum cart speed is 1.5 m/s at f=1/4. Momentum is the conserved numerator; kinetic energy is generally dissipated. An injector or interception geometry must actually deliver slower and faster material to the cart; a rear-only stream of slower material cannot catch it.

6. Nonreacting solution mixing

Let M₀ be initial solution mass, c₀ its solute mass fraction, m added solution mass, and c_in(m) feed solute mass fraction. Retain all inflow, with no reaction, evaporation, precipitation, or outlet:

\[c(m)=\frac{M_0c_0+\int_0^m c_{in}(s)\,ds}{M_0+m}.\]

Example: initial solution 0.25 kg at 5% solute by mass; reference added solution 2 kg; feed concentration 10%·f. The tank reaches 2.5% at f=1/4. Mass fractions avoid assuming that mixed volumes add. Inlet fractions must remain between zero and one.

7. Two ways the correspondence can fail

First, a tank with an outlet has a different accumulated denominator. Second, sources, losses, reactions, or external forces add new numerator terms.

Let S be a numerator source rate, W the current weight, and dots denote time derivatives. For a single inflow carrying U, without an outlet:

\[W\dot Y=\dot W(U-Y)+S.\]

With fixed total weight, equal inflow/outflow rate q, perfectly mixed outflow, and inlet U, the balance becomes W·dY/dt=q(U−Y)+S. A fixed-weight tank can show exponential relaxation. Our growing-weight mixture generally does not. The familiar resemblance is not sufficient to claim identical time dynamics.

Unknown unknowns—and how to resolve them

These are the conceptual traps most likely to survive even after the main derivation is understood. Each includes a way to check the claim.

The zero-mass shell is a singular limit

For any positive shell mass, the empty CG returns to b. If shell mass is exactly zero, the empty system has no CG. For the cylinder, taking fill to zero before shell mass gives β; reversing the limits gives zero. The limits do not commute.

\[\lim_{r\to0^+}\lim_{f\to0^+}y(f,r)=\beta,\qquad\lim_{f\to0^+}\lim_{r\to0^+}y(f,r)=0.\]

Check: move the mass-ratio slider toward its lower limit and examine the narrowing near-empty rise. A coarse plot can miss it.

A turning point has exceptional robustness

At the minimum, height of the last layer equals the current average. Small fill errors therefore affect CG only at second order. For a cylinder, let δf be a small fill perturbation and y_min the minimum response:

\[y(f_*+\delta f)=y_{\min}+\frac{(\delta f)^2}{2(r+f_*)}+O((\delta f)^3).\]

Check: sample fill symmetrically around the minimum. The first-order change cancels.

The optimum does not require a cylindrical bottle

The vertical-slice uniqueness theorem covers any fixed rigid orientation with a single bottom-filled water region. What changes is the map from height to volume. Check: vary the area slope and compare depth at the minimum with volume fraction at the minimum.

The experiment’s frame can dominate the signal

A bottle raised to your mouth moves bodily upward even while its CG moves downward relative to its shell. In an accelerating frame the liquid responds to effective body acceleration; rotation can add spatially varying centrifugal terms. Check: keep the bottle fixed and drain it through a tube before trying a drinking experiment.

The system boundary changes the answer

Bottle plus remaining water is an open system. Bottle plus water already swallowed plus the drinker is a different system, with a different CG. No paradox is resolved by applying closed-system momentum statements to only part of it. Check: write the included components before choosing a conservation law.

Low CG does not mean hardest to knock over

For a rigid body with a frozen mass distribution, centered CG height z and circular support radius R_b, the quasistatic tipping angle is arctan(R_b/z). The energy barrier to pivot the CG over an edge is ΔE=Dg[√(z²+R_b²)−z], where D is total mass. Thus minimum height and maximum energy barrier need not coincide. Liquid redistribution changes this rigid-body calculation further.

\[\theta_c=\tan^{-1}(R_b/z),\qquad \Delta E=Dg\left(\sqrt{z^2+R_b^2}-z\right).\]

Check: distinguish the tilt threshold, energy barrier, sliding threshold, and impulse needed to topple. They answer different questions.

CG cannot identify the entire mass distribution

Different distributions can share the same mass and first moment but have very different moments of inertia and slosh responses. Check: compare two solid mock bottles with the same mass and CG but different radial ballast placement.

A shared formula is weaker than shared physics

Thermal mixing dissipates temperature differences, capacitor sharing can dissipate electrical energy, and inelastic capture dissipates kinetic energy. The bottle moment calculation is geometric. Their normalized response equations agree under specific input protocols; their microscopic dynamics and entropy budgets do not. Check: list what is conserved and what is dissipated in each case.

You can infer bottle properties from a CG experiment

Let u*=h*/H and r=M/m_full for a cylinder. The critical condition rearranges to

\[\beta=u_*+\frac{u_*^2}{2r},\qquad r=\frac{u_*^2}{2(\beta-u_*)}.\]

If β is known, a measured turning depth estimates the mass ratio. If r is known, it estimates β. One turning point alone cannot independently identify both. Check: measure empty mass separately and compare the inferred β with a suspension measurement.

Differentiation amplifies noisy measurements

Finding the zero of a numerical derivative of measured CG can be unreliable. Fit the physical moment model to the raw data, estimate parameter uncertainty, then infer the minimum. Shared errors in height calibration or retained water are not cured by more repeated samples. Check: bootstrap repeated measurements and inspect residuals versus fill.

Apparent balance can include forces other than weight

External buoyancy, a supporting straw, surface contact, or an attached hose can change measured forces and torques without changing the material center of mass. Check: isolate contact forces in a free-body diagram; do not fit every balance shift as a shifted mass centroid.

“Very slow” has a timescale attached

Let T_d be the draining time and ω a characteristic slosh frequency. A large product ωT_d helps support a quasistatic approximation, but rapid individual sips or persistent low damping can still excite motion. Small capillary features can relax on other timescales. Check: wait for observed oscillations to decay after each change, rather than relying on the average drinking rate.

A practical diagnostic: when data depart from the model, first ask whether the mass distribution changed, the system boundary changed, the reference frame changed, or the measurement includes an extra force. These explain more than adding an arbitrary correction coefficient.

Three questions deserve especially deep treatment

These were singled out because each changes the reasoning framework rather than merely adding a small correction. They expose an everyday misconception, connect to a wider theory, and suggest a falsifiable experiment.

A. Stability: the objective comes before the optimum

“Hardest to knock over” is incomplete until we specify what is imposed: angle, force, work, impulse, or angular velocity. These have different units and different dependencies on mass and inertia. This question is especially valuable because it challenges the tempting inference that the lowest CG is automatically the most stable state. The next tab develops it in detail.

B. Inverse geometry: can a CG curve reveal the vessel?

Let h be settled water depth, z(h) the combined CG height, M the known empty-bottle mass, and L(h) the total remaining mass. Assume uniform density, a fixed upright interior, fixed shell CG, and no retained films. A prime denotes a derivative with respect to h. The forward balance identity can be inverted:

\[z\,L=Mb+\int_0^h s\,dL(s),\qquad Lz^{\prime}+zL^{\prime}=hL^{\prime},\qquad \frac{d\ln L}{dh}=\frac{z^{\prime}}{h-z}.\]
\[L(h)=M\exp\!\left[\int_0^h\frac{z^{\prime}(s)}{s-z(s)}\,ds\right],\qquad A(h)=\frac{L^{\prime}(h)}{\rho}.\]

Here b is the empty-shell CG, ρ the water density, and A(h) the horizontal interior area. This turns geometry → CG into CG → geometry. It identifies area versus height, not a unique three-dimensional interior: differently shaped horizontal sections can have the same area and the same CG curve. It is especially valuable because it separates uniqueness with perfect data from reliable inference with noisy data. The measured horizontal coordinate must be depth, not an unknown volume fraction. At h=z, both numerator and denominator vanish; with a smooth positive area, their continuous ratio tends to z″ at the turning point because z′=0 there. A single CG measurement cannot replace the full curve or these assumptions.

Why noise matters: the reconstruction differentiates measured data, divides quantities that can both be small, and then exponentiates an integral. Fit a physically constrained smooth forward model to data and quantify uncertainty rather than directly differentiating raw samples.

C. Conservation: the boundary is part of the physics

The bottle’s CG can rise while no remaining material moves upward: low material has left the chosen system. Include the transferred water and drinker, and it is a different system. Include the Earth when discussing support and gravity exchanges. A bottle alone is open during drinking and experiences external forces; an enlarged isolated system is the appropriate setting for a closed-system momentum statement.

Which fill makes the bottle hardest to knock over?

There is no universal best fill. For our running example with immobilized water, quarter-full maximizes critical tilt, full maximizes the lifting-energy barrier and the ideal impulse threshold at a fixed strike height, and about 30.7% full maximizes the required initial angular speed. Freely settling water shifts the critical-tilt optimum to about 25.7% full. These statements answer different experiments.

Read the model label on every visual. “Immobilized water” means a hypothetical rigid payload with the upright water distribution—such as a matched solid replica. It is not ordinary water remaining horizontal while the bottle rotates. “Settled water” means a sealed rigid cylindrical interior, fixed water volume, negligible air mass and capillarity, with enough time to equilibrate at every imposed angle. Neither model solves sloshing impacts.

2. Critical tilt: when does gravity stop restoring upright?

For the rigid-payload reference, measure horizontal and vertical CG position from the right rim pivot. Let x be horizontal displacement toward the falling side and Z height above the original support plane. Rotation gives

\[x(\theta)=-R\cos\theta+z\sin\theta,\qquad Z(\theta)=R\sin\theta+z\cos\theta.\]

Weight points vertically downward. Its torque about the rim is mgx in the tipping direction. When x is negative, gravity restores upright; when x is positive, it drives tipping. The boundary is

\[\theta_c=\tan^{-1}(R/z),\qquad \max_f\theta_c\iff\min_f z,\qquad f_{CG}=0.25\ \text{for the reference}.\]

The reference critical angle is 21.23° at quarter-full, versus 10.99° for either full or empty. Equal endpoint angles do not mean equal impact resistance. This is the release angle of a rigid payload on a fixed rim, or the rigid-body toppling boundary on a sufficiently rough inclined base. Sliding and free-liquid redistribution require the separate models below.

3. Energy: the height of the hill, not its angular location

Define U(θ) as gravitational potential energy and ΔU as the rise from upright to the unstable equilibrium. The rim constraint fixes the path. Since U=mgZ,

\[U(\theta)-U(0)=mg[R\sin\theta+z(\cos\theta-1)],\qquad \Delta U=mg\left(\sqrt{R^2+z^2}-z\right).\]

The cancellation-free form is useful for a slender bottle. Let d=√(q²+y²), and E=ΔU/(m₀gH) be dimensionless energy:

\[E(f)=(r+f)(d-y)=(r+f)\frac{q^2}{d+y},\qquad E^{\prime}(f)=\frac{d-y}{d}(d+y-f).\]

Unlike critical tilt, this barrier depends explicitly on total mass. For a midpoint-balanced shell, f−2y=r(f−1)/(r+f)≤0, so d+y−f is strictly positive for R>0. Therefore the energy barrier increases throughout 0 ≤ f ≤ 1: full is best. This is an exact statement within this rigid model, not merely a numerical plot.

General shell CG: when can the energy optimum be interior?

The monotonic result above uses β=1/2. For general β≥0, the positive stationary fill is

\[f_E=\min\left\{1,\ \beta+\frac{q^2}{2r}+\sqrt{\left(\beta+\frac{q^2}{2r}\right)^2+q^2}\right\}.\]

For positive r and q, the derivative starts positive and changes sign only at the unclipped positive root; it is the global maximum if inside the allowed interval. A sufficiently low shell CG can produce an interior energy optimum. At the boundary case β=0, it remains well defined, even though the upright CG minimum occurs empty. Do not extend the thin-sidewall inertia formula below to arbitrary β without specifying a new shell mass distribution.

4. A slow horizontal push: force at a specified height

Apply a horizontal force F at fixed initial height p on a level plane. At incipient tipping, force and weight moments about the rim balance. With a Coulomb static friction coefficient μ, sliding competes with tipping:

\[F_{tip}=\frac{mgR}{p},\qquad F_{slide}=\mu mg,\qquad F_{first}=mg\min(R/p,\mu).\]

Tipping begins before sliding only if R/p<μ. At fixed R and p, both thresholds increase with remaining mass, so the full bottle requires the greatest force for the first loss of static equilibrium. The upright CG height drops out of this onset calculation because the weight’s initial lever arm is R. This does not predict how far a continuously pushed, sliding bottle will travel or whether it will later strike an obstacle and fall.

A prescribed horizontal support acceleration is a different test. For an immobilized payload, a horizontal inertial force ma acts at the CG, giving a_tip/g=R/z and a_slide/g=μ. Mass cancels, so the best rigid tipping-acceleration threshold is again the lowest-CG fill. For slowly equilibrating liquid, the free surface tilts perpendicular to effective gravity; the upright-CG formula must be replaced by a corresponding fluid equilibrium calculation. Arbitrary earthquake-like or vibrational inputs require time dynamics.

5. A short strike: inertia and the input protocol matter

A horizontal impulse J is the time integral of strike force. In an ideal model the bottle rotates immediately about a fixed rim without slip, bounce, dissipation, or internal motion, and the strike is initially at height p. Angular momentum about the pivot gives L=Jp and I_Pω=L. After the impulse, energy conservation determines whether the crest can be crossed:

\[\frac{(Jp)^2}{2I_P}>\Delta U,\qquad J_{crit}=\frac{\sqrt{2I_P\Delta U}}{p},\qquad L_{crit}=\sqrt{2I_P\Delta U},\qquad \omega_{crit}=\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta U}{I_P}}.\]

Equality is a separatrix: in the ideal lossless equation the motion approaches the unstable upright-over-pivot state asymptotically; strict excess is needed to pass it. A force threshold cannot replace an impulse threshold without specifying the force’s duration. Nor can a fixed angular velocity be treated as a fixed impulse: inertia enters these criteria in opposite ways.

For the plotted reference, model the shell as a uniform thin cylindrical sidewall with massless retaining end caps, and the immobilized water as a solid cylinder. About the horizontal rim pivot, the parallel-axis theorem gives

\[I_P=M\left(\frac{3R^2}{2}+\frac{H^2}{3}\right)+m_0 f\left(\frac{5R^2}{4}+\frac{(fH)^2}{3}\right).\]

Both I_P and ΔU increase with f for this midpoint-shell model, so L_crit and J_crit at fixed p are greatest full. At p=25 cm, J_crit is approximately 0.0166 N s empty, 0.0437 N s quarter-full, and 0.1489 N s full. In contrast, maximizing ω_crit selects approximately 30.7% full for this geometry. That optimum depends on the shell’s second moment, not merely its CG.

A real short strike does not guarantee a fixed pivot or a rigidly moving liquid. Contact impulses, possible lift-off, liquid–wall coupling, and later slosh must be solved or measured. A high wall-friction force cannot be assumed to make water instantaneously rigid. The ideal impulse result is a controlled reference, not a calibrated prediction for an ordinary water bottle.

The angular-speed optimum can be found explicitly as a one-dimensional root. Define dimensionless inertia ι=I_P/(m₀H²) and angular speed Ω=ω√(H/g). With E and d defined above, an interior stationary fill requires the relative growth rates of energy and inertia to agree:

\[\Omega_{crit}^2=\frac{2E}{\iota},\quad \iota=r(3q^2/2+1/3)+f(5q^2/4+f^2/3),\quad \frac{E^{\prime}}{E}=\frac{d+y-f}{d(r+f)}=\frac{5q^2/4+f^2}{\iota}=\frac{\iota^{\prime}}{\iota}.\]

Solving this stationarity condition and comparing all roots with both endpoints gives f≈0.3065 for the reference dimensions. The energy is still increasing there; the angular-speed threshold turns downward because inertia is growing faster in relative terms. This is why the maximum is neither the minimum-CG fill nor the maximum-energy fill.

6. Let the water settle: the free surface changes the problem

A slowly tilted bottle contains water that remains horizontal in the laboratory. Use a sealed cylinder so volume is fixed and no water spills. Let s be axial distance from the internal bottom, x a horizontal coordinate across its circular section before rotation, and ℓ the laboratory free-surface height above the rim pivot. The water occupies

\[\Omega_w(\theta)=\{(x,y_{\perp},s):x^2+y_{\perp}^2\le R^2,\ 0\le s\le H,\ (R-x)\sin\theta+s\cos\theta\le\ell\}.\]

The transverse coordinate y_perp is distinct from the dimensionless upright CG y used above. Choose ℓ by the volume constraint ∫Ωw dV=fπR²H. The liquid CG and its potential energy follow from first moments over this occupied three-dimensional region. An exact formula before the surface reaches an end cap

When the free surface intersects every axial line inside the cylinder, local liquid depth is s_top(x)=fH+x tanθ. This requires R|tanθ|≤H min(f,1−f). Circular symmetry gives the area mean of x² as R²/4. Integrating depth and its first moment yields

\[\int_{\Omega_w}x\,dV=\frac{\pi R^4}{4}\tan\theta,\qquad \int_{\Omega_w}s\,dV=\frac{\pi R^2 f^2H^2}{2}+\frac{\pi R^4}{8}\tan^2\theta.\]

Define V=U/(m₀gH), the dimensionless absolute potential, and B=rβ+f²/2. For midpoint β=1/2 in the numerical lab, and for any fixed β in this analytic formula:

\[V_{rigid}=q(r+f)\sin\theta+B\cos\theta,\qquad V_{settled}=V_{rigid}-\frac{q^2\sin^2\theta}{8\cos\theta}.\]

The negative term is the free-surface energy reduction. At a given angle, liquid can lower its gravitational energy by redistributing; the frozen distribution is only one allowed competitor. More generally, minimization over liquid configurations proves the settled potential cannot exceed the frozen potential at the same angle. Since their upright energies agree, the settled barrier cannot exceed the rigid barrier over the same angle interval. This is a variational argument, not a claim that every dissipative transient follows the minimum instantaneously.

\[V_{settled}(\theta)=B+q(r+f)\theta-\frac12\left(B+\frac{q^2}{4}\right)\theta^2+O(\theta^3).\]

The extra negative curvature is the local free-surface destabilization. The same mechanism explains why a moving liquid can undermine the stability of a container even when its upright CG looks favorable. At exactly full or exactly empty, there is no partially filled free surface; the unclipped formula has no nonzero-angle range of validity and must not be extrapolated there.

Two deductions from the variational view

First, the greatest settled-water energy barrier is also attained full for this midpoint-shell cylinder. At every partial fill its settled barrier is no larger than its rigid barrier; every partial rigid barrier is below the full rigid barrier; and at full there is no free surface, so the settled and rigid results agree. The full-water energy winner therefore survives relaxation, although the critical-angle winner shifts.

Second, the critical angle is unique for the ideal sealed cylinder. For any fixed candidate liquid configuration, potential has the form g(a sinθ+c cosθ), where a and c are nonnegative mass moments about the rim and the positive shell contribution makes the potential strictly concave on 0≤θ≤π/2. Minimizing over admissible liquid configurations preserves concavity. The potential derivative starts positive and ends negative, so there is one crest. This justifies the bracketed critical-angle solve; it does not justify using the same solver for a different contact geometry or spilling vessel.

Derive the best settled-water fill in the unclipped regime

At the critical angle the potential derivative vanishes. Differentiating its zero condition with respect to fill shows that an interior optimum in critical angle must also satisfy f=q cotθ. Substitution gives

\[\frac{f^2}{2}+rf-r\beta-\frac{q^2}{4}-\frac{q^4}{8f^2}=0,\qquad \theta_{best}=\tan^{-1}(q/f).\]

For r=1/8, β=1/2, and q≈0.0971154, the root is f=0.256678 and θ=20.7244°. The extreme local depths are 0.21993H and 0.29342H, so the unclipped assumption is self-consistent. 10. What this teaches beyond the bottle

A state variable such as CG is a useful compression, but it cannot answer every question about a system. Stability needs a disturbance and a constraint; energy barriers need the whole path; impulse response needs inertia and contact; freely moving liquid adds internal degrees of freedom. The general habit is to ask which observable the model is actually sufficient to predict.
